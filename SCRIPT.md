# "The Train That Left Before It Arrived" — original script v1 (60 s, 9:16 Shorts)
Format: the reference-Clips-style short (~60 s, ~3.7 words/s), the voice, our own characters + story.
Facts: In the 1880s, every American town set its clocks by local solar noon, so noon in Pittsburgh was noon but noon in Cleveland was ~11 minutes later. Railroad timetables printed per-town times, creating impossible schedules where a train could appear to arrive before it departed. The confusion helped drive the adoption of four standard time zones ("railroad time") on November 18, 1883, known as "The Day of Two Noons." Sources: general railroad/time-zone history (standard time adopted by US railroads 1883-11-18; local solar time prevailed before).

Hook: A train left the station before it even arrived there.
Setting: It's 1883, and every American town runs on its own sun time. Noon in Pittsburgh is noon; noon in Cleveland is eleven minutes later.
Escalation 1: The railroad prints timetables anyway, one per town, each swearing its own noon is the real one.
Escalation 2: A train leaves Cleveland at eleven forty-nine and arrives in Pittsburgh at eleven forty — before it left.
Escalation 3: Passengers watch one train pull out as an identical train pulls in, and nobody knows which clock to trust.
Escalation 4: Conductors carry three pocket watches, passengers miss connections, and a fruit train arrives rotten because "noon" came twice.
Mystery: Wait — how can a train leave before it arrives?
Pivot: You see, every town set its clocks by the sun, so noon traveled west at a walking pace — about a mile every four minutes.
Analogy: Imagine you're on that train, your watch clicking noon while the next town still says eleven forty-nine, and you've gained time just by sitting still.
Consequence: The chaos forced America into four standard time zones — railroad time, imposed by the tracks themselves.
Callback twist: So now, every train leaves after it arrives — because we finally agreed what noon means.

Target length: ~60 s at ~3.7 words/s (~216 words). Voice starts at frame 1. No captions unless asked.
